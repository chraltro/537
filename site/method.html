How it works, and how well

Every number on this site comes out of one pipeline that runs from public match results to a simulated league table. This page explains each step and then shows what happens when the model is made to predict the past without being allowed to see it.

How current is this?

The build stamp on every page says when the pipeline last ran. That is not the same as how fresh the data is, and on this site the two are routinely weeks apart: the results mirror does not create a season's file until months into it (it added 2025-26 on 17 February 2026), and openfootball's own in-season commits come roughly weekly with multi-week gaps. So this says what the pipeline could actually see.

Is it calibrated?

A forecast can rank teams correctly and still lie about how sure it is. The test: gather every outcome the model called x% likely, and check how often those actually happened. Points on the diagonal mean the stated confidence is honest.

The model

The engine is a time-weighted Dixon–Coles model. Every club gets an attack rating and a defence rating, which are exactly the two quantities FiveThirtyEight's Soccer Power Index was built from: how many goals a club would be expected to score, and concede, against an average team on neutral ground. A correction term handles the well-known fact that 0–0 and 1–1 happen more often than independent scoring rates imply. Recent matches count for more, with a decay that gives a match a half-life of roughly one season.

Fitting on shots as well as goals

The model is fitted twice, once on goals actually scored and once on the goals a club's shot profile implies, and the two rating sets are blended. This is the single most important idea borrowed from SPI. Over a handful of matches, goals are dominated by finishing luck, while shot volume and shot quality repeat. A club that has been creating chances and not scoring is usually about to start scoring.

A free data feed has no shot locations, so the shot-implied figure here is coarse: a league-wide conversion rate fitted on 20,000 matches, worth about 0.23 goals per shot on target and a small fraction of that for shots off target. It is a stand-in for expected goals, not the real thing.

How much does it actually buy? The grid search that set the model's two parameters says: a little. Blending shots in is worth roughly 0.005 of log-loss. That is real, but far less than a proper expected-goals model would give, and less than the gain from simply weighting recent matches more heavily.

Rating clubs that have never played in this league

Every season brings up clubs with no record in this division at all. Rather than guess, the model is fitted across the top flight and the division below it together. Clubs are promoted and relegated every year, so the two divisions are stitched into one rating scale by the clubs that have played in both.

That alone would flatter promoted clubs, so the correction is measured rather than assumed. Taking every promoted club since 2013 (39 of them) and comparing the rating they arrived with against the rating they went on to earn gives two numbers:

  • a level shift: promoted clubs perform meaningfully below what their second-tier form implies. The step up is real and it is large.
  • a flattening: the gaps between promoted clubs barely survive the jump. Winning the second tier at a canter says much less about the season ahead than it feels like it should.

Established clubs, by contrast, carry most of their rating from one season to the next.

What results cannot see

A results model is blind to a new manager, a £90m striker, or a squad sold off over the summer. Before a ball is kicked, that blindness is the model's biggest weakness, so the forecast is anchored to the preseason betting market: the simulation is asked what rating adjustment would reproduce the market's title and relegation prices, and takes a fraction of it.

The anchor is temporary by design. Its weight falls to zero over the first ten matchweeks, as the season's own results become better evidence than any preseason opinion. The odds snapshot, its capture date and its sources are checked into the repository, so anyone can see exactly what was used. Simulating the season

Match probabilities come from the exact score distribution: an 11×11 grid of every plausible scoreline. The season is then played out 50,000 times, with results already on the board carried in as fact.

One detail matters more than it looks. Between simulations the model redraws each club's rating from its uncertainty, not just each match from its probability. Without that, a forecast says football is random but our ratings are perfect, and produces confident nonsense, with narrow points ranges and title races that are over in November. Ties are settled the way the league does it: points, then goal difference, then goals scored.

What a match is worth

Every simulated season also records what happened in each remaining fixture, which makes a second question nearly free to answer: given a home win, how often does each club end up champion, in the top five, or relegated, and how does that differ given an away win? The gap between those two numbers is what the site calls the swing, and it is the honest measure of whether a fixture matters. Two mid-table sides with nothing to play for score near zero however famous they are; a relegation six-pointer between two poor teams scores near the top.

Half time, cards and referees

Every row of the results feed has carried a half-time score, the referee's name, cards, corners and fouls since 2000-01, and until recently this project read none of them. The half-time score now gets its own Dixon–Coles fit, the same model on a different target. That is where the interval probabilities on each match page and the game-state figures on each club page come from. Goals only, because shot counts are full-match totals and cannot be split across the break.

Cards and fouls are published as a club's record, not as a model input. The referee is published the same way and deliberately does no work: no source reachable from the build names the official before kick-off, so a referee term could not be applied to a match that has not been played. A referee who books more is also usually working harder fixtures rather than being stricter. The column is also, in practice, a Premier League one: twenty-six seasons carry it there, against two in the Bundesliga files, two in Serie A's and none at all in La Liga's or Ligue 1's. So the referee section below appears for the Premier League and nowhere else.

The cross-league rating

The global ranking is a second, separate fit: one Dixon–Coles model over fifteen seasons of UEFA competition, the big five, every non-big-five top flight with a feed, and the Championship. That comes to about sixty-six thousand matches and eight hundred clubs. The continental matches are the only edges joining one league to another, and they are what identify each league's offset. It carries a home term per competition, and it shrinks each club toward its own league's mean rather than toward the global one. Without that, a pooled fit would drag Real Madrid down and Lincoln Red Imps up by the same amount, and what came out would not be a rating of anything.

That rating is published on its own page and on its own scale, and it does not feed the league forecasts here. SPI means "share of points against an average opponent", and pooling redefines the average as the average of eight hundred clubs across fifty-three leagues; swapping one for the other would move every number on this site without a single new result behind it.

Competitions with only one feed

Five of the eight competitions on this site come from the football-datasets mirror, which carries shots, cards and half-time scores. The mirror has exactly five league directories, so the Eredivisie, the Primeira Liga and the Championship are read from openfootball alone: goals and half-time scores, no shots, no cards, no referee, and no second source if that feed goes quiet. The shot-and-goals blend already handles a club with no shot data, since it is the same mechanism that rates a promoted club, but those three forecasts lean entirely on goals. If one of those feeds stalls, the freshness panel at the top of this page is where it shows up.

Marking its own homework

Scoring a forecast against results it has already absorbed is meaningless. So each match's probabilities are archived the last time the model saw it as unplayed, and that record is never overwritten once the game kicks off. The in-season figures at the top of this page are computed only from those frozen forecasts.

What it does not do

  • No injuries, suspensions or lineups. A club missing its first-choice striker looks the same to this model as one at full strength. This is the largest single gap.
  • No true expected goals. Shot locations are not in any free feed, so shot quality is approximated by whether a shot was on target.
  • No live anything. The pipeline runs every six hours, so there is no in-play win probability and there never will be from these sources.
  • No referee effect. Referee appointments are not published anywhere the build can reach before kick-off, so the referee page is a record and not a prediction.
  • No fixture congestion or European commitments. A Thursday night in Baku followed by a Sunday lunchtime kick-off is not modelled.
  • Promoted clubs carry real uncertainty. The correction is measured across 39 past cases, but 39 is not a large sample, and clubs differ.
  • It is not betting advice. The model has no access to team news and does not try to beat a bookmaker.

Data and code

Match results, shots and cards come from datasets/football-datasets, a daily-updated mirror of football-data.co.uk. Fixtures and second-tier results come from openfootball. The forecast rebuilds itself every six hours on GitHub Actions. Model, site and the odds snapshot are all in the repository.
